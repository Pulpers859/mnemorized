## Limits

Generator exhaust is an exposure-context reminder, not uniquely carbon monoxide.
Do not depict the gas itself as visible colored smoke. The source's airborne
droplet phrase is vocabulary, not an endorsed contemporary infection-control
taxonomy. Tsunami lung is a name cue, not a diagnosis or complete explanation.
Gauntness, struggling, soot and separated bone ends are not diagnostic criteria.
No medicine, procedure, dose, threshold or causal chain is being generated.

Structured scope/reasons: [candidate bank](chapter-6-cue-audit.json).
## Independent Review

An independent AI reviewer checked the 17 proposed inclusions and held term
against the source. All page matches were supported, with the headword
normalizations noted above. The reviewer found no outright rejection under the
broadened standard, but flagged that wedding rings alone suggest union and an
outside scaffold could read as a generic brace. Main revised the bone characters
to visibly turn away with folded arms and made the scaffold-to-bone connection
explicit. These final wording refinements were main's integration, not a second
independent render audit.

The reviewer also flagged generator-fume ambiguity, vector-arrow familiarity,
and the broadness of hunger/removal/water-distress categories. These remain
retained learned-context candidates, with limitations preserved in the bank.
Review was of source text and proposed cues; prior-bank consistency was checked
by main. It was not a credentialed clinical review, blind image-naming test, or
empirical learning study. No rendered readability was tested.

## User Addition After Initial Review

The original run retained13 and held sulfonylureas. Patrick then proposed rotten
eggs through their familiar sulfur association. Retained as a partial learned
bridge to sulfon-, not a full-word sound match. This corrects the overly narrow
assumption that no whole-word pun means no useful cue. Stink lines make the
rotten state visually apparent. This is not a claim about the drug's odor,
chemistry, allergy cross-reactivity or clinical effect. Other sulfur-related
terms can share the primitive; resolve those by lesson context rather than
rejecting the association. Original review findings above remain historical;
the new candidate is user-originated and still untested.
